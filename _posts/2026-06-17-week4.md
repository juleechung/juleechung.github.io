I started attending [PARCS Lab](https://parcslab.fyi/) meetings and began testing frontier vision-language models on museum photos I took while pointing at objects. The goal was to see how well current models resolve a person's deictic gesture in visually dense, object-rich scenes.

The museum tests were useful because they exposed a familiar failure mode: models often identify the general area or a salient object, but they do not always follow the pointing geometry precisely. In paintings and galleries, a small change in angle can change the intended referent from a vessel to a plate, a figure's hat, or a detail in the background.

<p>Across every still, the models resolved the gesture by picking whatever sat nearest my fingertip in the image. Arm direction barely counted. That works when the referent lies under the finger and fails when it does not, which is what the dog and the feathered hat both show. Walls with several paintings were the hardest case. The video clips were the exception, because a moving hand gave the model a direction to follow.</p>

These tests helped clarify why the project should not rely on a frontier model's answer alone. A strong HRI system needs an explicit representation of the gesture, the candidate objects, and the uncertainty in the model's interpretation. It also needs a way to ask for clarification when several plausible referents remain.
